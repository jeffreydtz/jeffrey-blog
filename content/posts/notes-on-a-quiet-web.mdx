The first website I ever loved belonged to a stranger. It was a hand-built page about lighthouses — I don't remember how I landed there, only that it had a visitor counter, a page of links to other lighthouse people, and a note that said _last updated_ followed by a date months in the past. Nobody was trying to convert me into anything. The page just sat there, patient, like a house with the lights on.

I've been thinking about that page a lot lately, mostly because of how rarely the feeling repeats. The web I use every day is extraordinary in a hundred measurable ways and exhausting in one unmeasurable one: everything is loud. Every surface wants something from me — a subscription, a reaction, a fraction of a second of scroll velocity. Even personal sites, when you find them, often arrive dressed as businesses: the newsletter modal, the sticky bar, the testimonial wall. The house became a storefront.

## What quiet actually was

It's easy to get nostalgic in the wrong direction here, so let me be precise. The old web wasn't better because it was ugly, or slow, or hard to use. It was better — in this one narrow sense — because it was **illegible to metrics**. The lighthouse page had no way of knowing whether I stayed four seconds or an hour, and so it had no reason to be designed around either number. Its only possible audience was a person, reading.

That illegibility produced a particular texture. Pages could be odd. They could be about one thing forever. They could go months without updating and feel dormant rather than dead, because nothing about them promised a schedule. A quiet page makes a different contract with the reader: _I made this because I wanted it to exist. Stay as long as you like._

The loud web makes the opposite contract, and we can all feel it, even when we can't name it. You know within half a second whether a page wants to be read or wants to be measured. Your shoulders know it before you do.

## Building a small house

Which is, in a roundabout way, why this site exists. I wanted to find out whether the quiet contract could still be signed in 2026 — whether I could build something with modern tools that still behaves like the lighthouse page. Not as a retro exercise; I have no interest in fake visitor counters or pixel fonts. The nostalgia worth keeping is structural, not aesthetic.

So the rules I set for myself were about behavior, not looks:

- **No metrics that change decisions.** I genuinely don't know how long you've been here, and the design can't be optimized against a number I refuse to collect.
- **Everything is a file.** Each essay is a text file in a folder, versioned like code. If every service I depend on disappears tomorrow, the writing survives on my disk, readable in any editor.
- **Nothing interrupts.** No popups, no banners, nothing that slides in. The most aggressive thing on this site is a footnote.
- **Slow by design.** Essays over updates. Months of silence are a feature; the _last updated_ date is allowed to get old with dignity.

None of this is original — there's a whole loose movement of people rebuilding [small, self-owned corners of the web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IndieWeb), and this site owes plenty to essays of theirs I've collected over the years. What I can add is only testimony: it feels different to write for a quiet page. Knowing there's no dashboard waiting changes the sentences. I catch myself writing longer paragraphs, fewer hooks. It turns out the metrics were shouting at me too, not just at you.

I don't think the loud web is going anywhere, and I still live there most of the day, like everyone. But I've started to think of pages like this one as houses on a street that never quite emptied — odd little buildings with the lights on, each maintained by one person for no defensible reason. If you have a spare evening sometime, build one. Put anything on it. Lighthouses, if you like.

Leave the lights on. Somebody will find it.
